# Ireland

**Official name**: Ireland (*Éire*)
**Capital**: Dublin (*Baile Átha Cliath*)
**Founded**: Constitutional settlement 1652 (Lambert's land settlement); domestic Assembly established through the 1660s–1670s; full partnership in the United Nations of Great Britain and Ireland, 1707
**Head of Government**: Chief Minister (*An Príomh-Aire*), elected by the Irish Assembly
**League membership**: No independent Maritime League or League of Nations seat; represented through the United Nations of Great Britain and Ireland, with its own delegation in League councils by the late 18th century

---

**Official languages**: Irish (*Gaeilge*) and English, co-official
**Government**: Self-governing nation within the United Nations of Great Britain and Ireland

---

## Overview

Ireland is the least commercially prominent and, by most outside estimation, the most quietly distinctive of the United Nations' three member nations. It is the only one of the three where a majority Catholic population holds effective political weight in its own governance, the only one governed for most of its history by a landed gentry rather than a merchant class, and the only one where the pre-conquest vernacular survived as a living language of law, learning, and daily life rather than retreating to folk memory. None of this was inevitable. All of it traces to a single decision made in the settlement's first years, and to the fact that no one has ever fully agreed on why it was made.

Ireland joined the United Nations of Great Britain in 1700 and became an equal partner under the Act of Union of 1707, a full half-century after Lambert's Commonwealth first constituted itself and a full generation behind Scotland's compact. The delay reflected reality rather than exclusion: Ireland in the 1650s was a conquered territory administered from London, not a negotiating partner, and the road from that condition to equal partnership had to be walked rather than declared. That it was walked at all, without the recurring violence that so often accompanies such transitions, is the country's central historical fact.

## The Settlement That Wasn't

The Cromwellian conquest — the campaigns of 1649 and 1650 — was complete before Lambert assumed leadership of the Commonwealth at Worcester. What he inherited was not a war but a reckoning still to be written, and the reckoning the Rump Parliament had been drafting would have confiscated some eleven million acres of Irish land, roughly two-thirds of the country, on the standing presumption that every Catholic landowner was a rebel unless he could prove otherwise — with even the proven innocent to be removed west of the Shannon regardless.

Lambert's settlement, drawn narrower, confined confiscation to those who had actually borne arms against the Commonwealth: the officers, the commanders, the instigators of the 1641 rising. Catholic landowners who had stayed neutral or submitted kept their estates. The transplantation scheme was never enacted. Parliament's war creditors — the Adventurers who had financed the conquest against warrants on Irish land, and the unpaid soldiers who expected the same currency — were still paid; roughly five million acres changed hands rather than eleven. But an indigenous Catholic gentry remained in possession of the land it had held before the war, and a class of people with an estate to protect does not, as a rule, burn it down.

Why Lambert drew the line where he did remains an argument rather than a settled fact, and it is an argument conducted differently on either side of the Irish Sea. The reading favoured in much Irish historiography treats it as principle: a man who had already committed himself to governing through law rather than force could not, having made that commitment, preside over the wholesale legal dispossession of a people on the basis of collective guilt. The reading more common among English constitutional historians is colder: a Commonwealth barely two years old, still absorbing the shock of Cromwell's death, could not have sustained the permanent military occupation that a fully dispossessed and transplanted population would have required, and Lambert — a soldier before he was anything else — understood the arithmetic of that occupation better than anyone in the Rump drafting the confiscation schedules. Both readings agree on what he did. They do not agree on whether Ireland was spared out of conviction or out of cost, and Lambert's own writings, characteristically, offer support for either conclusion without settling the matter.

The distinction may matter less than it appears to. Whatever the reasoning, the practical result was the same: Ireland kept a propertied Catholic class with a stake in stability, and the republic was spared a garrison it could not have afforded. Historians who spend their careers on the question are, in effect, arguing about Lambert's character using a decision whose consequences would have been identical either way.

## Language and the Gentry

The transplantation's absence had a consequence that outlived every political settlement built on top of it: the communities that would have been forcibly relocated stayed where they were, and the language they spoke stayed with them.

Gaelic Ireland's learned classes — the poets, brehon-trained lawyers, and hereditary historians whose institutions had already been weakening before the conquest — did not disappear the way a fully executed transplantation would have scattered them. The Catholic gentry that kept its land largely kept its bilingualism too: Irish for the household, the parish, the local courts of arbitration and custom that continued to settle disputes the way they always had; English for dealings with Dublin Castle's administration and, later, for the United Nations. Neither language displaced the other, because neither was ever forced to.

This produced a durable diglossia rather than the slow retreat of a defeated vernacular. Irish remained the working language of a very large share of the rural population well into the modern era, not as a preserved heritage practice but as the ordinary language of markets, courts of first instance, and home life, alongside English as the language of union-level government, higher commerce, and international correspondence. By 2000, Ireland is unusual among the world's smaller nations in that its pre-conquest language did not need reviving, because it was never allowed to lapse in the first place. The Gaelic Academy in Dublin — founded in the eighteenth century to standardise Irish orthography for the growing volume of printed legal and commercial material in the language — is treated less as a monument to loss than as an ordinary institution doing ordinary administrative work, in the way a weights-and-measures office is ordinary.

## Ireland's Long Road

Ireland's accession to the United Nations of Great Britain in 1700 and the renegotiated Act of Union of 1707 — which renamed the body the United Nations of Great Britain and Ireland, moved the secretariat to Berwick, and established unanimity for major decisions — are covered in full in `world/18th-century.md`. What bears repeating here is the sequence's internal logic from Ireland's own side: a Catholic gentry with property and standing, an Anglo-Irish settler population that had come to want the same domestic autonomy for its own reasons, and a century of pamphlets and assembly debate making the case that a republic — unlike a Catholic monarchy claiming to be the faith's natural protector while persecuting it — asked nothing of a Catholic's conscience that his loyalty to Rome contradicted. By the time the question came to a vote in Westminster, it was less a question of whether to admit Ireland than of how long England could keep finding reasons to delay.

The Catholic officeholding bar at the union level was not lifted until the 1707 Act. Ireland's own domestic Assembly had never observed an equivalent restriction — Catholic gentry sat in it from its earliest sessions in the 1660s and 1670s, since the bar had only ever been an English Commonwealth rule applied to the Commonwealth's own offices, not a condition Ireland's internal settlement imposed on itself. The result, for a working generation, was the peculiar arrangement of Catholics who could sit in the Irish Assembly in Dublin but not represent Ireland at Berwick — a distinction Irish members found difficult to explain to constituents and impossible to defend on principle, and one of the standing grievances the 1707 settlement was built to resolve.

## Constitutional Structure

### The Irish Assembly

The Assembly (*An Tionól*) sits in Dublin and holds authority over Ireland's domestic affairs — law, taxation, land, courts, and the church settlement — while foreign policy, defence, and currency are shared through the United Nations. Its franchise developed along broadly propertied lines, comparable in structure to England's own, though its composition was never restricted by faith: Catholic and Protestant landowners alike qualified on the same property terms from the Assembly's founding.

### The Chief Minister

*An Príomh-Aire*, elected by the Assembly and answerable to it, holds executive authority over domestic administration. The office carries no fixed term of the kind the Instrument of Government imposes on England's Lord Protector, nor the age-based retirement provision — the Assembly's power to withdraw confidence at any sitting has, in practice, done the same work.

### Representation at Berwick

Ireland sends its delegation to the United Nations secretariat on equal footing with England and Scotland since 1707: full veto on major decisions, simple-majority participation in routine business. Irish delegates have, since the Act of Union, been reliably drawn from the Catholic gentry and professional classes far more often than from the smaller Protestant landholding population — a proportion roughly tracking the country's own religious composition, which English and Scottish observers alike have long since stopped finding remarkable.

## Internal Factions

### The Catholic Gentry
The dominant political class since the Assembly's founding — landed, propertied, historically wary of the Commonwealth that conquered their forebears and settled comfortably enough into governing under its rules once those rules proved survivable. Broadly conservative on land and church matters, increasingly confident in Ireland's standing within the union as each generation passes without a renewed confiscation.

### The Anglo-Irish
Descendants of the Adventurers and soldiers settled under the Commonwealth's land settlement, and of the earlier Elizabethan and Jacobean plantations before them — Protestant, English-descended, but born and raised in Munster and Leinster rather than England. Wanted Irish domestic autonomy for reasons distinct from the Catholic gentry's but arrived at the same practical position: local governance suited them better than administration from London or Berwick.

### The Ulster Presbyterians
Descendants of the Scots plantation settlers in the north, Presbyterian rather than Anglican or Catholic, historically closer in religious sensibility to the Scottish Kirk than to either of Ireland's other major communities. A persistent, distinct bloc rather than a swing constituency — its interests align with the Catholic gentry on resisting London-centralising proposals and with the Anglo-Irish on commercial and trade matters, depending on the question.

### The Dublin Merchants
A smaller but growing commercial class oriented toward union-level and League trade rather than land, concentrated in Dublin and the eastern ports. Politically the least predictable of the four blocs, and the one most likely to align with whichever of the others currently favours expanding Ireland's commercial standing within the United Nations.

## Society

Ireland by 2000 remains predominantly Catholic, with a substantial Protestant minority divided between the Anglo-Irish gentry and professional classes and the Ulster Presbyterian community in the north. Irish and English are both used in government, education, and commerce, with the balance between them varying by region and occasion rather than following a hard administrative line. The country's economy, historically agrarian and land-based, industrialised later and less completely than England or the Dutch Republic, a fact Irish economic historians attribute to the gentry-led constitutional settlement's own conservatism as much as to any external constraint.

## Position in the United Nations and the League

Ireland's formal standing runs entirely through the United Nations of Great Britain and Ireland — it holds no independent seat in the Maritime League or its successor, the League of Nations. In practice, Irish delegates have participated in League councils in their own right since the late eighteenth century, generally in informal coordination with the Republic of Meuse and other Catholic republican members on questions touching religious establishment, a natural alignment given Ireland's own settlement history. Irish representatives were among those who ensured Napoleon Bonaparte's ambitions toward the Papal States were firmly redirected during the Italian settlement of the 1790s, judging correctly that the political cost of disturbing the Papacy fell disproportionately on Catholic constituencies within otherwise Protestant or secular republics.

Within Berwick's own councils, Ireland's veto carries the same formal weight as England's or Scotland's, though its practical influence has historically lagged England's commercial and Scotland's military reputations. What Ireland has instead is patience: a domestic settlement built slowly, tested repeatedly, and never yet broken since the day Lambert chose five million acres over eleven.
