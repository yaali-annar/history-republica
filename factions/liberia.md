# Liberia

**Official name**: The Republic of Liberia  
**Capital**: Christopolis  
**Founded**: 1847 (federation of the Province of Freedom and the Mesurado settlements)  
**Head of Government**: President of Liberia, elected by Congress for a fixed term  
**League membership**: Maritime League (1847), League of Nations (successor membership)

**Government**: Federal republic  
**Constituent states**: The Province of Freedom, Mesurado, the Gallinas, and the six Hinterland Provinces admitted in 1931  
**Official languages**: English. Krio, Mende, Temne, Kpelle, and Vai are recognised in provincial courts and assemblies.

---

## Overview

Liberia is a federal republic on the Upper Guinea coast of West Africa, stretching from the Freetown peninsula in the north to the Grebo coast at Cape Palmas in the south, and inland across the forest belt to the edge of the Futa Jallon highlands. With the Cape Republic at the continent's southern tip, it is one of the two republics through which the League has held its place in Africa. Its settlements predate the Covenant, and for most of the nineteenth century it was the League's only substantial presence on a coast where the Iberian Empire held territory on both flanks.

Its first citizens came from three directions. Free Black emigrants from the Union of States settled at Cape Mesurado. People freed by the League's anti-slaving squadron from captured Iberian ships were landed at Freetown. The peoples of the coast and forest had lived there for centuries before either group arrived. The republic's history is largely the story of these three communities learning, slowly, what they owed one another.

Liberia in 2000 is stable, moderately prosperous, and proud of a constitutional record that its own historians describe with more candour than most. Its elected Congress is dominated by representatives from the interior provinces. Its Council of Chiefs is one of the League's more studied constitutional experiments. It has never been conquered, and it has never had a coup.

## The Freed and the Franchise

The founding document of the Union of States abolished permanent hereditary bondage in 1763. It did not give the freed a vote. The Union's franchise was propertied, and a population that had owned nothing, including itself, entered the republic with no property to qualify on. By the 1790s the southern states held a generation of free Black citizens who had been born free, worked their whole lives in a republic that called itself a government by consent, and had never been asked for theirs.

The idea of emigration to Africa gathered two constituencies that agreed on almost nothing else. The first was Black-led: congregations of Black Methodists and Baptists in the southern port cities, and a small class of free Black merchants and mariners in the north, who argued that a people shut out of one republic could found another. The second was a portion of the southern planter interest, which had lost its slaves in 1763 and would have preferred to lose the freed population as well. The Society of Friends financed the project anyway, on the view that a free republic in Africa was worth building whatever the motives of some who helped pay for it.

The **African Settlement Society**, chartered in Philadelphia in 1799, held the coalition together. It never resolved the question of what it was for. Liberian political writing has been arguing about which of its two founding motives was the real one for two hundred years, and the argument has never been settled because both were real.

The man who made the project practical was **Paul Cuffe**, a Black shipowner of Wampanoag and African descent from Massachusetts, raised among the Friends. His voyages of 1804 to 1808 carried the first emigrant families to the Upper Guinea coast, negotiated land at Cape Mesurado from the Dei and Bassa chiefs of the region, and established the settlement that its first residents named **Christopolis**.

The name was the settlers' choice, not the Society's. Cuffe's Quaker sponsors in Philadelphia had proposed something plainer, and the Friends were uncomfortable with a town named for Christ in the way they were uncomfortable with steeple-houses and titles of honour. The settlers were Methodists and Baptists who had just crossed an ocean to reach a promised land, and the Friends declined to press the point. The republic's later name, **Liberia**, was the compromise both traditions could accept: plain, abstract, and Latin enough to satisfy the Society's more classically minded members.

## The Province of Freedom

The English settlement at Freetown was older, and it began for different reasons.

The English Republic's Caribbean and Guiana colonies still held enslaved people in the late eighteenth century, and its merchant fleet was still a carrier in the Atlantic trade. English abolitionists such as **Granville Sharp** argued from the Union's example: if a republic founded by English colonists could abolish hereditary bondage at its birth, the mother republic had no excuse. The **Province of Freedom** was founded at the Sierra Leone estuary in 1787 by Sharp's circle, settled by free Black Londoners and freed people from the English Caribbean, and governed at first by a chartered company that had more idealism than capital.

It would probably have failed without the Convention.

The **Convention of The Hague** (1792) committed the members of the Maritime League to end the slave trade by their own ships and to treat it as piracy in League waters. Its real target was the one great carrier left: the Iberian Empire, then building the Brazilian and Cuban plantation economies that would absorb most of the Atlantic trade for the next seventy years. See `world/18th-century.md`.

Enforcing the Convention required a base on the African coast, and Freetown had the best natural harbour between the Gambia and the Gold Coast. The **West Africa Squadron**, English at first and later a joint League command with Dutch, Union, and French vessels, was stationed there from 1794. Its ships intercepted Iberian slavers along the coast. The people it freed could not be returned to homes hundreds of miles inland from which they had been taken, and most were landed at Freetown.

These **Recaptives** transformed the Province of Freedom. Across the following six decades roughly ninety thousand people were landed there: Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Kongo, and people from dozens of other nations, sharing nothing but the ship that had carried them. Settled in villages on the peninsula and taught in mission schools, within two generations they had become a people of their own: the **Krio**, speaking a Creole English rich in Yoruba, trading along the whole West African coast, and educating their children at **Fourah Bay College** (1827).

The Squadron's other legacy was the **Kru**. The Kru of the southern coast had been seafarers before any European ship arrived. They became the Squadron's indispensable crews and pilots, then the crews of League merchant ships across the Atlantic, on their own terms. Neither Freetown nor Christopolis ever quite governed them.

## The Gallinas Question

Between Freetown and Christopolis lay the Gallinas coast: a stretch of lagoons and river mouths, invisible from the sea, which by the 1820s was the largest slave-trading station on the Upper Guinea coast. Its barracoons were run by Iberian factors, the most notorious of them **Pedro Blanco**, with the support of Gallinas chiefs who profited from the trade and the backing of the Iberian governor at Bissau. After the Colonial Protocol of the 1820s, Iberia treated supplying its plantation economies as a Covenant obligation, and the Gallinas was how it met that obligation.

The Squadron's captains wanted to go in and burn it. They were not permitted to. The Convention made the trade piracy at sea. It gave no right to land on a coast whose chiefs were sovereign and had signed nothing with anyone. The English Republic's African policy rested on the same principle as its Asian one, that the League traded and treated but did not take land, and the lawyers of the English Admiralty were not going to set a precedent that Iberia could quote back at them in the Caribbean. From 1839 the Squadron did what the law allowed: it stationed ships off the Gallinas bar and took every slaver that crossed it.

Iberia escalated. It produced treaties, signed by Gallinas chiefs, placing the coast under Iberian protection. Then, in 1842, it sent warships from Bissau to escort the slavers out. It was the first time a Covenant navy had escorted the trade against a League squadron, and it left the Squadron's captains watching slave ships sail past under a flag they could not fire on without starting a war. Liberian historians date the long Iberian hostility on the Guinea coast from that year.

The question was now a legal one, and harder to answer than it looked. The Province of Freedom was an English possession, but the Gallinas lay outside it. Christopolis was not a state at all, only a settlement belonging to a Philadelphia society. If the coast between the two settlements was not a state's, Iberia's treaties would do, and a Covenant protectorate on the Gallinas would divide the League's two West African footholds permanently. Only a state could sign competing treaties with the Gallinas chiefs, and only a state could close the barracoons by law.

The answer was federation. The English Republic, which had been paying for the Province of Freedom for half a century and had no appetite for a colonial war on the Guinea coast, agreed to release it on the model it had already applied to the Union of States and that the Dutch Republic had applied to the Cape. The Union of States, whose citizens had founded Christopolis but whose government had carefully never claimed it, recognised the settlers' right to constitute a state. The Gallinas chiefs had found that Iberian protection brought escorts for the factors' ships and nothing for themselves, and they negotiated terms. The **Constitution of Liberia** was proclaimed at Christopolis on 26 July 1847. The Maritime League admitted the new republic in the same year, on the sponsorship of England and the Union of States. The republic's first statute closed the Gallinas barracoons, and the Iberian factors left under the escort of the same warships that had been protecting them.

## A Republic on the Coast

The Constitution of 1847 was, in almost every clause, the Union of States' constitution adapted to a smaller country. It created a Congress of two houses, a President elected by that Congress for a fixed term, and a federal division between three constituent states: the Province of Freedom, Mesurado, and the Gallinas. It placed the capital at Christopolis. And it adopted the Union's franchise, which is to say a property qualification.

The Settlers who drafted it had left a republic whose property qualification had excluded them. They wrote the same qualification into their own constitution, because it was the only republican government they had known. Its effect was to exclude the Krio majority of the Province of Freedom, most of whom were poorer than the Settler families of Christopolis, and almost everyone who lived outside the coastal towns.

The Freetown Krio noticed that a republic in which they were the largest community had chosen a capital across the Gallinas in the town where the qualified voters lived. The rivalry between the two capitals has outlasted every constitutional reform since. Freetown has the harbour, the League naval station, and Fourah Bay. Christopolis has Congress and **Liberia College** (1862). Each regards the other as provincial.

### The Treaty Chiefdoms

The deeper question was the interior, and here the Settlers reached for the other model the Union had given them.

The Union of States had never governed the Haudenosaunee. It had renewed the Covenant Chain, a treaty relationship between recognised peoples, and the Settlers had grown up in a republic that took some pride in that. Liberia applied the same logic to the peoples of the forest belt. The Mende, Temne, Kpelle, Gola, Loma, Kissi, Limba, and Vai chiefdoms beyond the coastal strip were not annexed. They were **Treaty Chiefdoms**, bound to the republic by agreements of friendship, trade, and mutual defence, governed by their own paramount chiefs under their own law, and neither citizens of Liberia nor its subjects.

For half a century the arrangement worked better than its critics expected. The chiefdoms kept their autonomy, the republic kept its trade routes, and the Muslim Mandinka and Fula merchants who carried the interior's commerce moved between them under the protection of both.

The arrangement also reproduced, on African soil, the exclusion that had driven the Society of Friends out of England. The Friends had been free to worship and hold property and had been barred from civic life only by the oaths it required. The peoples of the Treaty Chiefdoms were free, protected, and permanently outside the republic that claimed to be their friend. Liberian historians began making this comparison in the 1890s. Their Quaker correspondents in Philadelphia did not dispute it.

### The Vai Franchise Case

In the 1830s a Vai scholar, **Momolu Duwalu Bukele**, devised a syllabary for writing the Vai language. Within a generation it was in wide use among Vai traders, entirely independent of any mission school.

The Constitution of 1847 allowed a literacy qualification as an alternative to property in municipal elections, and it did not say literacy in what. In 1868 a group of Vai merchants from the Gallinas, literate in Bukele's script and in Arabic but not in English, presented themselves to register in Robertsport. The registrar refused them. The **Vai Franchise Case** reached the Supreme Court of Liberia the following year, and the Court held that the Constitution's literacy clause meant literacy in any written language. The ruling opened the municipal franchise, and over the following decades the national one, to Vai and Mandinka merchants and to the Muslim scholars of the interior towns. It was the first breach in the coastal monopoly on the franchise, and it was made not by the Settlers' generosity but by an African script that its inventor had created without reference to them.

## The Iberian Grudge

Iberia never recognised the Gallinas settlement. Its maps showed the coast as an Iberian protectorate until 1905, and its escorted slave convoys kept working the Guinea coast for another two decades, beyond Liberian waters and under the Squadron's eyes. Twice in the 1850s escort and squadron exchanged fire. Both governments recorded the incidents as accidents.

What turned a territorial grievance into a lasting grudge was Brazil. Most of the Recaptives landed at Freetown were Yoruba, and so were many of the enslaved of Bahia, carried across the same ocean from the same coast, often from the same towns. Krio traders had kin in Bahia and wrote to them. When the Brazilian uprisings of the 1850s and 1860s began, the network that John Brown drew on ran mostly through Ayiti, but one of its strands ran through Freetown: letters, money, and news carried by Krio merchants and Kru crews along routes the Iberian authorities could see but not close. After Brown's capture in 1863, the Iberian case against the League named Freetown alongside Port-au-Prince.

Iberia could punish the Union of States through the nitrate price. It had no such lever over Liberia, whose economy needed nothing Iberia sold. What it had was Bissau and the Liberian interior. From the 1860s Iberian agents paid subsidies and supplied arms to interior chiefs with grievances against the coast and to Gallinas families who still wanted their trade back. None of it amounted to war, which was the point. It was the beginning of the pattern that put Iberian rifles in the hands of the Hinterland War's fighters thirty years later.

## The Scramble and the Hinterland

Until the 1870s Iberia's African energies went into the Pink Strip, and the Guinea coast was a place for slave factories rather than administration. The **Protocol Revision** of 1874 changed that by giving Prussia **Kamerun** on the Bight of Biafra: the Covenant was now competing for West Africa rather than merely trading on it. Iberian governors at Bissau pushed inland, and Iberian agents, joined now by Prussian ones, stepped up the treaties and rifles they had been offering the chiefs of the Liberian interior since the 1860s.

The **Lisbon Conference** of 1884 then adopted the Covenant doctrine that territory not effectively administered by a recognised state was open to claim (see `world/19th-century.md`). The League rejected it as applied to its African partners. It could not reject it on behalf of the Treaty Chiefdoms, because the whole premise of the treaty model was that the chiefdoms were not Liberia's to administer. A chief who signed an Iberian treaty was, by Liberia's own constitutional theory, a sovereign exercising his right to choose his friends.

Some did. Through the late 1880s and 1890s a belt of chiefdoms on the northern margin, where Iberian influence from Bissau now reached through the Futa Jallon, signed Iberian treaties, took Iberian subsidies, and armed with Iberian rifles. Gallinas families who had never forgiven the closing of the barracoons joined them. Their grievances were real: the coast's trade terms, the Krio traders' reach into the interior, the property franchise that told them they would never be citizens of the republic they were told was their friend. Iberia supplied the rest.

The **Hinterland War** began in 1898, when the Iberian-aligned chiefdoms attacked the Krio trading posts on the Rokel and Scarcies rivers and then moved south against the Mende and Temne chiefdoms that had kept faith with their Liberian treaties. The aim, as Iberian correspondence later made plain, was to create an interior that Liberia visibly did not control, and so a claim under the Lisbon doctrine. The war was fought as much between interior chiefdoms as between the interior and the coast. The Mende heartland and most of the Temne chiefdoms fought alongside the Liberian Frontier Force, and it was their levies, not the Frontier Force, that broke the northern alliance in 1899. It was ugly on every side, and it remains the only war Liberia has fought on its own soil.

It ended in the **Bo Accord** of 1899, and the Accord was the allied chiefs' as much as the republic's. The chiefs who had carried the fighting drew the conclusion that the treaty model could not survive the Lisbon doctrine: a chiefdom Liberia did not govern was a chiefdom Iberia would keep trying to claim. They agreed to bring the interior under the republic's jurisdiction, keeping their own courts and customary law, in return for joint defence and a promise of representation in the republic's government "when the provinces are prepared for it." The **Hinterland Ordinance** of 1900 put the Accord into law. The defeated northern chiefdoms were brought under it on the same terms, which their neighbours insisted on and the coast accepted.

The interior was not conquered. It came in by agreement, under the pressure of a war the Covenant had armed. What the coast did with that agreement is the part Liberian historians find harder to write about. The promise of representation took thirty-two years to keep, and the standard school text on the period describes the delay as "the debt the republic owed to the chiefs who saved it, and paid a generation late."

## After the Covenant

The Covenant's collapse in 1905 ended the external threat that had driven Liberia inland. Iberian Bissau lost its garrison and then its governor. Prussian Kamerun, cut off from Iberian shipping, was placed under a League transitional commission in which Liberian civil servants, Krio administrators from Freetown above all, played a larger part than Liberia's size would suggest.

It also ended the argument that the interior had to wait. The Bo Accord's promise could no longer be deferred on grounds of security, and the Mende and Temne chiefs, whose sons had now been educated at Fourah Bay and in Ayiti, said so. The **Constitution of 1931** admitted six **Hinterland Provinces** as constituent states on equal terms with the coastal three, abolished the property qualification for federal elections, and created the **Council of Chiefs**. Universal suffrage followed in 1946.

The twentieth-century economy was built on the interior: rubber from a Philadelphia company's concession of 1926, diamonds from the Kono fields from the 1930s, and iron ore from the Nimba range from the 1950s. The diamonds brought the republic's worst corruption crisis, a smuggling network of the 1960s and 1970s that ran money into the Soledad Box. It ended with prosecutions and the **Diamond Accord** of 1978, under which the League audits the Kono revenues annually at Liberia's own request.

## Constitutional Structure

### The President

The President of Liberia is elected by Congress sitting in joint session for a single term of six years and may not be re-elected. The office follows the Union model closely: the President heads the executive, conducts foreign policy, and commands the Frontier Force and the coast guard, and is answerable to Congress for all of it. By unwritten convention since the 1960s, a President from the coastal states is followed by one from the Hinterland Provinces, and the reverse.

### Congress

Congress has two houses. The **House of Representatives** is apportioned by population, and since 1946 has had a permanent majority from the Hinterland Provinces. The **Senate** gives each of the nine constituent states three senators regardless of population, which gives the three coastal states a weight in the Senate out of all proportion to their share of the electorate.

### The Council of Chiefs

The Council of Chiefs sits alongside Congress but is not part of it. Its members are paramount chiefs, chosen by the chiefdoms of each Hinterland Province according to their own customary procedures. It has no power to initiate legislation. It holds a suspensive veto over any law touching customary land tenure, chieftaincy, marriage and inheritance under customary law, or the treaties of the nineteenth century, which Liberia's courts still treat as binding instruments.

The model was borrowed openly from the Northern Realm's practice of incorporating traditional authority into constitutional government, and from the Nusantara Federation's accommodation of its sultanates. Liberian constitutional writers point out, with some satisfaction, that the Council is the only institution in the republic whose members were never required to meet a property qualification.

## Society

### The Settlers

The descendants of the emigrants from the Union of States call themselves Settlers, and have done so for so long that the word has lost whatever edge it once had. They number about five percent of the population, concentrated in Christopolis, the Mesurado towns, and the St. Paul River settlements. Their churches (Methodist, Baptist, and the Christopolis African Methodist Episcopal congregations) are among the oldest institutions in the republic. They speak a Liberian English that visitors from Philadelphia find either charming or archaic. Settler families dominated the republic's politics until 1931 and its professions for long afterward.

### The Krio

The descendants of the Recaptives and of the Province of Freedom's first settlers are the Krio, about eight percent of the population, concentrated in Freetown and the peninsula villages. Krio, their language, is the republic's common tongue: spoken as a first language by the Krio and as a second by most Liberians of every other community, it is the language of the markets, of popular music, and increasingly of the national press. The Krio have been, for most of the republic's history, its traders, clerks, lawyers, and civil servants. The Settler and Krio communities are often grouped together by interior politicians as "the coast." Neither community accepts the grouping.

### The Peoples of the Interior

The great majority of Liberians belong to the peoples of the former Treaty Chiefdoms. The Mende and the Temne are the largest groups, followed by the Kpelle, the Limba, the Loma, the Kissi, the Gola, and many smaller nations. The Vai of the Gallinas coast and the Mandinka and Fula communities of the interior towns are predominantly Muslim, and Islam is the faith of roughly a third of the republic's population. The **Kru** and **Grebo** of the southern coast are counted among the interior peoples for constitutional purposes and regard themselves as belonging to neither category.

### Liberia and Ayiti

Liberia and Ayiti, the League's two oldest Black republics, have been closely tied since the nineteenth century. The University of Saint-Domingue has educated a large share of Liberia's interior leadership since the 1880s, and the Mende and Temne politicians who forced the Constitution of 1931 were Ayitian-educated almost to a man, which Settler commentators of the period did not fail to point out.

## Position in the League

Liberia was the League's second African member, after the Cape Republic, and remained its only member in West Africa until after the Covenant's fall. For most of the nineteenth century it was the League's indispensable station on the Guinea coast: the Squadron's base, the anchor of its relationships with the Asante, the Yoruba states, and Sokoto, and the proof that the republican order could take root in Africa.

In 2000 Liberia is a mid-sized League member with a regional weight that exceeds its size. Freetown remains the League's principal naval station in West Africa, and the Kru mariners' tradition has made Liberia one of the League's larger seafaring nations. Its relationship with the Cape Republic, the League's other African foothold, is warm and somewhat competitive.

Its relationships with its two sponsors have both matured. The English Republic, which released the Province of Freedom in 1847, is Liberia's largest trading partner. The Union of States, whose citizens founded Christopolis, is its oldest friend and its most frequent source of irritation, mostly through the Philadelphia rubber company, which Liberians of every community regard as the Union's least attractive export.
