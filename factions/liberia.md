# Liberia

**Official name**: The Republic of Liberia  
**Capital**: Christopolis  
**Founded**: 1847 (from the settlements of the Sierra Leone Company, chartered jointly by the English Republic and the Union of States)  
**Head of Government**: President of Liberia, elected by Congress for a fixed term  
**League membership**: Maritime League (1847), League of Nations (successor membership)

**Government**: Federal republic  
**Constituent states**: The Province of Freedom, Mesurado, the Gallinas, and the six Hinterland Provinces admitted in 1931  
**Official languages**: English. Krio, Mende, Temne, Kpelle, and Vai are recognised in provincial courts and assemblies.

---

## Overview

Liberia is a federal republic on the Upper Guinea coast of West Africa, from the Freetown peninsula south to Cape Palmas and inland to the edge of the Futa Jallon. With the Cape Republic, it is one of the League's two republics in Africa, and for most of the nineteenth century it was the League's only substantial presence on a coast where the Iberian Empire held territory on both flanks.

It began as the **Sierra Leone Company**, a joint enterprise of the English Republic and the Union of States. Its people came from three directions: free Black emigrants from the Union, Africans freed from Iberian slave ships and landed at Freetown, and the peoples of the coast and forest who had lived there for centuries.

Liberia in 2000 is stable and moderately prosperous. Its Congress is dominated by the interior provinces, a Council of Chiefs sits alongside it, and the interior chiefdoms govern themselves under customary law. It has never been conquered and has never had a coup.

---

## The Sierra Leone Company

The Union of States abolished hereditary bondage in 1763 but kept a property franchise, and its freed population entered the republic with nothing to qualify on. By the 1790s a generation of free Black citizens in the southern states had been born free and had never voted. Emigration to Africa drew two constituencies with opposite motives: Black Methodist and Baptist congregations and free Black merchants who wanted a republic of their own, and southern planters who wanted the freed population gone. The Society of Friends financed it regardless.

In England the pressure ran the other way. The English Republic's Caribbean and Guiana colonies still held enslaved people, and abolitionists such as **Granville Sharp** argued that if the Union could abolish bondage at its birth, the mother republic had no excuse. In 1787 Sharp's circle founded the **Province of Freedom** at the Sierra Leone estuary with free Black Londoners and freed people from the English Caribbean. It had money and very few settlers.

The two movements needed each other: London had capital and the navy, Philadelphia had the emigrants, and the Friends' meetings on both sides of the Atlantic had corresponded for a century. In 1791 the Province was reconstituted as the **Sierra Leone Company**, chartered jointly by the English Parliament and the Union Congress, with boards in London and Philadelphia. The Union, whose own colonies had been fused by joint customs and a combined payment to two metropoles, saw nothing unusual in governing a settlement from two capitals. The first Union emigrants reached Freetown in 1792.

The **Convention of The Hague** of the same year bound League members to end the slave trade by their own ships and to treat it as piracy in League waters. Its practical target was the Iberian Empire, then building the plantation economies of Brazil and Cuba. A joint English and Union **West Africa Squadron**, later joined by Dutch and French ships, was based at Freetown from 1794. The people it freed from Iberian slavers could not be returned to homes deep in the interior, and most were landed at Freetown.

These **Recaptives** transformed the Province. Some ninety thousand were landed over six decades: Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Kongo, and dozens of other nations. Within two generations they had become a people of their own, the **Krio**, speaking a Creole English rich in Yoruba, trading along the whole coast, and educating their children at **Fourah Bay College** (1827). The Squadron also made the fortune of the **Kru** of the southern coast, seafarers who became its crews and pilots and later the crews of League merchant ships across the Atlantic.

With the peninsula filling, the Philadelphia board looked south. The **African Settlement Society** (1799) recruited emigrants in the Union, and the ships of **Paul Cuffe**, a Black Quaker shipowner of Wampanoag and African descent from Massachusetts, carried them. Between 1804 and 1808 Cuffe negotiated land at Cape Mesurado from the Dei and Bassa chiefs and founded the company's second settlement. Its Methodist and Baptist settlers named it **Christopolis**, over the objection of the Quaker directors, who disliked naming a town for Christ as they disliked steeple-houses and titles. The later name of the republic, **Liberia**, was the compromise.

## The Gallinas Question

Between Freetown and Christopolis lay the **Gallinas**, a coast of lagoons and river mouths that by the 1820s was the largest slave-trading station on the Upper Guinea coast. Its barracoons were run by Iberian factors, **Pedro Blanco** the most notorious, with the support of the Vai chiefs of the coast and the backing of the Iberian governor at Bissau. After the Colonial Protocol of the 1820s, Iberia treated the supply of its plantations as a Covenant obligation, and the Gallinas was how it met it.

The Squadron could not land. The Convention made the trade piracy at sea, not a right to occupy a sovereign coast, and the company's sponsors held to the League principle of trading and treating rather than taking land. From 1839 the Squadron blockaded the Gallinas bar instead. Iberia escalated: it produced treaties placing the Gallinas under Iberian protection, and in 1842 sent warships from Bissau to escort the slavers out, the first time a Covenant navy had escorted the trade against a League squadron.

The blockade was only half of the Gallinas chiefs' problem. The coast was squeezed from both ends. Krio traders from Freetown worked down from the north and Settler traders from Christopolis worked up from the south, and between them they took the palm oil and camwood trade that the Gallinas might have turned to once the slave trade closed. Iberian protection brought escorts for the factors' ships and nothing for the chiefs.

The company, for its part, could not answer Iberia's treaties. It held its settlements by purchase and treaty, and neither sponsor had ever claimed sovereignty over them. If the Gallinas belonged to no state, Iberia's treaties would stand, and a Covenant protectorate would divide the two settlements permanently. The company's boards agreed to wind up the charter and constitute its settlements as a sovereign state, as England had done with the Union in 1763 and the Dutch Republic with the Cape in 1784. The Gallinas chiefs, with no trade left to defend, negotiated terms and brought the coast into it. The **Constitution of Liberia** was proclaimed at Christopolis on 26 July 1847, and the Maritime League admitted the republic that year on the joint sponsorship of England and the Union. Its first statute closed the barracoons.

## A Republic on the Coast

The Constitution of 1847, drafted at Christopolis, followed the Union's in almost everything: a Congress of two houses, a President elected by Congress, and three constituent states, the Province of Freedom, Mesurado, and the Gallinas. The Gallinas was the smallest, Vai and largely Muslim, and its politics were shaped by the two larger Christian communities on either side of it. The capital was placed at Christopolis, where the convention sat. Freetown kept the harbour, the naval station, and Fourah Bay, and the rivalry between the two cities has outlasted every reform since.

### The Service Franchise

The one clause that owed nothing to the Union was the franchise. The Settlers had left the Union because its property qualification excluded them, and the Krio had arrived with even less. The convention took its model from the Republic of Meuse, whose guild franchise asked what a citizen did rather than what he owned. The **service franchise** gave the vote to any adult resident with three years' residence, a completed term of service to the commonwealth, and the ability to read the Constitution. For men the term was a year in the militia, followed by the reserve rolls until the age of forty-five. Those who could not bear arms served sixty days of labour on roads, harbours, and defences. A Recaptive landed with nothing could vote within three years.

The republic never had conscription. Most men on the coast served their militia year to earn the vote, and by the 1860s the reserve rolls held the greater part of the coast's adult men, drilled, armed, and organised by district. The Krio outnumbered the Settlers and outvoted them in the House, and the two communities' rivalry became ordinary politics.

### The Treaty Chiefdoms

The franchise stopped at the republic's borders. The Mende, Temne, Kpelle, Gola, Loma, Kissi, and Limba chiefdoms of the interior were not annexed. Following the Union's Covenant Chain with the Haudenosaunee, they became **Treaty Chiefdoms**, bound to the republic by agreements of friendship, trade, and mutual defence, and governed by their own paramount chiefs under their own law.

The arrangement suited the chiefdoms. To a Mende or Temne chief, a Settler was an English-speaking Christian from across the ocean, and a Recaptive was a Yoruba or Igbo stranger who had taken the Settlers' religion and language. Sharing the chiefs' colour did not make either less foreign. The chiefdoms wanted the republic's trade and its guns against their enemies, not its laws, churches, or citizenship. For half a century the treaties held, and the Muslim Mandinka and Fula merchants who carried the interior's commerce moved between the chiefdoms and the coast under the protection of both.

### The Vai Franchise Case

In the 1830s the Vai scholar **Momolu Duwalu Bukele** devised a syllabary for the Vai language, and within a generation it was in wide use among Vai traders. The service franchise required a voter to read the Constitution without saying in what language. In 1868 Vai merchants of the Gallinas, with the residence and the militia service but literate in Bukele's script and Arabic rather than English, were refused registration at Robertsport. In the **Vai Franchise Case** the following year, the Supreme Court held that the literacy clause meant any written language. The ruling opened the franchise to the Vai, Mandinka, and Muslim scholars of the coastal states, and became the precedent the interior chiefs later cited.

## The Iberian Grudge

Iberia never recognised the loss of the Gallinas. Its maps showed the coast as an Iberian protectorate until 1905, its escorted convoys worked the Guinea coast for two more decades, and twice in the 1850s escort and squadron exchanged fire.

Brazil deepened the grudge. Most of Freetown's Recaptives were Yoruba, as were many of the enslaved of Bahia, and Krio traders had kin there. When the Brazilian uprisings of the 1850s and 1860s began, the network John Brown drew on ran mostly through Ayiti, but one strand ran through Freetown, carried by Krio merchants and Kru crews. After Brown's capture in 1863, Iberia's case against the League named Freetown alongside Port-au-Prince.

Iberia could punish the Union through the nitrate price. It had no such lever over Liberia, and it could not attack the coast: every voter in Freetown and Christopolis was a militia reservist, and a landing would have met most of the coast's adult men under arms within days. The commanders at Bissau told Lisbon so more than once. From the 1860s Iberia worked through the interior instead, subsidising and arming chiefs with grievances against the coast and Gallinas families who wanted their trade back.

---

## The Scramble and the Hinterland

The **Protocol Revision** of 1874 gave Prussia **Kamerun**, and the Covenant began competing for West Africa rather than merely trading on it. Iberian governors at Bissau pushed inland, and Iberian and Prussian agents offered treaties and rifles to the chiefs of the Liberian interior. The **Lisbon Conference** of 1884 declared that territory not effectively administered by a recognised state was open to claim. The League rejected the doctrine for its African partners but could not reject it for the Treaty Chiefdoms, whose whole premise was that they were not Liberia's to administer. A chief who signed an Iberian treaty was, by Liberia's own theory, a sovereign choosing his friends.

Through the 1880s and 1890s a belt of northern chiefdoms, within reach of Iberian influence through the Futa Jallon, did so. Gallinas families who had never accepted the closing of the barracoons joined them. Their grievances were the coast's trade terms, the Krio traders' reach inland, and the Gallinas squeezed between Freetown and Christopolis.

The **Hinterland War** began in 1898 when the northern alliance attacked the Krio trading posts on the Rokel and Scarcies rivers. The reserve was called out within the week, and Krio militia companies held the posts. The alliance then turned south against the Mende and Temne chiefdoms that had kept their treaties, aiming to show an interior Liberia did not control and so to give Iberia a claim under the Lisbon doctrine. The war was fought as much between chiefdoms as against the coast. The Mende and most Temne chiefdoms fought beside the Frontier Force, and their levies broke the northern alliance in 1899. It remains the only war fought on Liberian soil.

In the **Bo Accord** of 1899 the allied chiefs concluded that the treaty model could not survive the Lisbon doctrine, since any chiefdom Liberia did not govern was one Iberia would keep trying to claim. They brought the interior under the republic's jurisdiction for defence and foreign relations only, kept their own courts, law, and government, and received a promise of representation "when the provinces are prepared for it." The **Hinterland Ordinance** of 1900 enacted the Accord, and the defeated northern chiefdoms were brought in on the same terms at their neighbours' insistence. The promise of representation was not kept until 1931.

---

## After the Covenant

The Covenant's collapse in 1905 ended the Iberian threat. Bissau lost its garrison and then its governor. Prussian Kamerun passed to a League transitional commission in which Krio administrators from Freetown played a leading part.

It also ended the case for deferring the Bo Accord's promise. The Mende and Temne chiefs, whose sons had been educated at Fourah Bay and in Ayiti, asked not for assimilation but for representation strong enough to protect the chiefdoms' autonomy. The **Constitution of 1931** admitted six **Hinterland Provinces** as constituent states on equal terms with the coastal three, made the chiefdoms their self-governing units, extended the service franchise inland, and created the **Council of Chiefs**. Universal suffrage followed in 1946.

The twentieth-century economy was built on the interior: rubber from a Philadelphia company's concession of 1926, diamonds from the Kono fields from the 1930s, and iron ore from the Nimba range from the 1950s. A diamond smuggling network of the 1960s and 1970s, which ran money into the Soledad Box, produced the republic's worst corruption crisis. It ended with prosecutions and the **Diamond Accord** of 1978, under which the League audits the Kono revenues annually at Liberia's request.

## Constitutional Structure

### The President

Elected by Congress in joint session for a single six-year term. The President heads the executive, conducts foreign policy, commands the militia, the Frontier Force, and the coast guard, and answers to Congress. By unwritten convention since the 1960s, Presidents alternate between the coastal states and the Hinterland Provinces.

### Congress

The **House of Representatives** is apportioned by population and has had a Hinterland majority since 1946. The **Senate** gives each of the nine states three senators, which gives the three coastal states a weight well beyond their share of the electorate.

### The Militia

The **militia** is the republic's army, and there has never been conscription. Militia service remains the usual route to the franchise for men, and most serve their year and pass onto the reserve until forty-five. The small standing **Frontier Force** is the militia's cadre and the interior's constabulary. Liberia can put a large part of its adult male population under arms within days.

### The Chiefdoms

The chiefdoms of the Hinterland Provinces are self-governing. Each has a paramount chief chosen by customary procedure and its own courts, and governs land, marriage, inheritance, and local disputes under customary law. The Poro and Sande societies keep their recognised place in chiefdom life. The provinces handle roads, schools, and federal revenue. The republic handles defence, foreign relations, currency, and the federal courts.

The Constitution's **Declaration of Rights** binds every chiefdom court. Pawning for debt, trial by ordeal, and forced marriage are prohibited. Any person may appeal from a chiefdom court to the federal courts on a question of constitutional right, and a person who moves to the coast is governed by federal law. The line between the Declaration and customary law is the most frequently litigated question in Liberian constitutional law.

### The Council of Chiefs

The Council of Chiefs sits alongside Congress but is not part of it. Its members are paramount chiefs chosen by the chiefdoms of each Hinterland Province. It cannot initiate legislation but holds a suspensive veto over laws touching customary land tenure, chieftaincy, customary marriage and inheritance, and the nineteenth-century treaties, which the courts still treat as binding. It was modelled on the Northern Realm's incorporation of traditional authority and the Nusantara Federation's accommodation of its sultanates.

## Society

**The Settlers**, descendants of the Union emigrants, are about five percent of the population, concentrated in Christopolis, the Mesurado towns, and the St. Paul River settlements. Their Methodist, Baptist, and African Methodist Episcopal churches are among the republic's oldest institutions.

**The Krio**, descendants of the Recaptives and of the Province's first London settlers, are about eight percent, concentrated in Freetown and the peninsula. Their language is the republic's common tongue, the second language of most Liberians and the language of the markets, of popular music, and increasingly of the press. Settler and Krio families between them dominated politics until 1931 and the professions long after.

**The peoples of the interior** are the great majority, and most live under chiefdom government and customary law. Many identify first with their chiefdom and people. The Mende and Temne are the largest groups, followed by the Kpelle, Limba, Loma, Kissi, Gola, and many smaller nations. The Vai of the Gallinas and the Mandinka and Fula of the interior towns are predominantly Muslim, and Islam is the faith of about a third of Liberians. The **Kru** and **Grebo** of the southern coast are counted among the interior peoples for constitutional purposes.

Liberia and **Ayiti**, the League's two oldest Black republics, have been closely tied since the nineteenth century. The University of Saint-Domingue has educated much of Liberia's interior leadership since the 1880s, including most of the Mende and Temne politicians behind the Constitution of 1931.

---

## Position in the League

Liberia was the League's second African member, after the Cape Republic, and its only West African member until after 1905. Through the nineteenth century it was the League's station on the Guinea coast: the Squadron's base and the anchor of its relations with the Asante, the Yoruba states, and Sokoto.

In 2000 it is a mid-sized League member. Freetown remains the League's principal naval station in West Africa, and the Kru seafaring tradition makes Liberia one of the League's larger maritime nations. Its relationship with the Cape Republic is close. The English Republic is its largest trading partner. The Union of States is its oldest partner, though the Philadelphia rubber concession has been a recurring source of friction.
