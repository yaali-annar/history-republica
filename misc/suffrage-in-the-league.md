# Suffrage in the Maritime League: A Comparative Note

*Reference entry, Straits Federal District Institute for Republican Studies, revised 1997. Compiled for the Institute's ongoing series on the constitutional development of League founding and early members.*

---

## Four Founding Logics

No two League founders answered the franchise question the same way, and the differences were not accidental — each answer was the specific solution to the specific problem of legitimacy each republic faced at its founding.

**England** asked *what you own*. The Instrument of Government set a property threshold because Lambert's Commonwealth needed to reassure a landed and commercial class, nervous about a republic's capacity for order, that a material stake in the settlement would govern who held a voice in it.

**The Dutch Republic** asked, in practice, *who you are*. The regent oligarchy that dominated Holland's cities through the seventeenth century filled council seats by co-optation among established merchant families — a franchise of standing rather than a franchise of test. It took the 1668 accession of Flanders and Brabant, and the guild traditions their cities carried into the States-General, to begin prying the Dutch franchise open toward anything broader.

**The Republic of Meuse** asked *what you do*. Built on Liège's centuries-old guild politics, the Meuse franchise ran on demonstrated service — a completed apprenticeship, a registered trade, a turn in the civic militia — and made no reference to property or birth at all. It was, in its first decades, the broadest franchise in the League, and the one most closely studied by republican thinkers who had no guild tradition of their own to build on.

**France** asked *what you register*. The Estates-General generation inherited the Meuse question but not the Meuse institutions, and built its own answer around the form of service its own membership understood: ownership or operation of a registered business. See `factions/french-republic.md` for the fuller account of how directly this borrowed from Liège, and how long it took France to build its own equivalent of a guild apprenticeship in the Brevet Civique's competence examination.

## The Liège Export

The Meuse model did not stay in Meuse. Dutch guild representatives in the newly admitted Flemish and Brabantine cities pushed franchise reforms that subsequently spread to the older Dutch provinces. French Enlightenment visitors to Liège carried the underlying principle — earned participation rather than inherited or purchased participation — back to Paris, where it surfaced first as the business-ownership franchise and later, more fully, as the Brevet Civique. Of the League's founding members, only England's franchise developed with no discernible debt to Liège's example, and English constitutional writers of the eighteenth century said so themselves, usually while arguing against adopting it.

## The Long Widening

By the middle of the nineteenth century, every League founder had extended its franchise well past its founding terms, and by the early decades of the twentieth, all had reached universal suffrage. The path each state took to get there, however, ran through a common obstacle — and it was not the one an outside observer familiar only with the founding terms might expect.

Sex was not the League's great suffrage battle. France had settled the question, more by legislative accident than principled resolution, a full century earlier: the Widow's Provision's ungendered drafting, ratified in law by 1801 and practiced for a decade before that, meant that by the time other League members debated extending their own franchises, a large republic had already been letting women vote for longer than anyone had bothered to argue about it. The Brevet Civique's own deliberately sex-neutral drafting reinforced the precedent rather than establishing it. Other League parliaments occasionally revisited the question rhetorically, but none treated it as a live constitutional fight the way France's own Assembly nearly had — the fight had already happened elsewhere, and had already been resolved by the time each state got around to asking.

The recurring fight instead was over competence — the worry, raised in nearly identical language in Westminster, The Hague, and Liège across the eighteen-hundreds, that a franchise extended to the propertyless and unschooled would hand the republic's decisions to people unequipped to make them. This was the Brevet Civique's own founding anxiety, translated into a dozen other legislatures' terms: property and business qualifications were not really about property or business at all, defenders argued as the qualifications came under pressure, but about ensuring the franchise stayed in hands capable of exercising it responsibly.

The resolutions varied by state, but the shape of the argument did not. Some — the Dutch cities, following the pattern the Guild Interest had set decades before — expanded the franchise well ahead of expanding public education, and let the education catch up afterward. Others, France's own departmental settlement chief among them, only reached full universal suffrage after a crisis discredited the elite's claim that its judgment could be trusted unsupervised in the first place. England's expansion was the slowest and most incremental of the founders', proceeding by repeated downward revision of the property threshold rather than any single dramatic reform, but it arrived at the same destination by the same century's end.

## The Realm's Anomaly

The state that reached full universal suffrage first was not one of the four founders at all. The **Northern Realm** — not a republic, not even a Maritime League member until the League of Nations superseded it — got there before England, before the Dutch Republic, before Meuse, and well before France.

Two things explain it, and neither is a franchise philosophy in the sense the founders would recognise. The Riksdag's historic Peasant Estate meant ordinary agrarian people had a formal seat in Swedish deliberation for centuries before "universal suffrage" was a phrase anyone needed — there was no gate to storm, because the gate had never quite closed. And the election of the first female High Guardian, forced by nothing more than a Bernadotte line running out of male heirs, settled the question of women's competence at the very top of the state before it was ever seriously litigated at the ballot box. See `factions/northern-realm.md` for the fuller account.

The Realm's own diplomats were candid, when pressed by larger states looking for reassurance, that scale had done at least as much work as principle. A country the size of Sweden and Norway combined could extend its franchise and watch what happened; a country the size of France was extending its franchise to a population large enough that any given failure of judgment, anywhere in it, would be found and publicised. The Northern Realm's example was, for exactly this reason, an uncomfortable one for the Technocrats' argument in Paris — if a smaller state could do it without catastrophe, "our state is too large to risk it" started to sound like an excuse for a preference rather than a genuine constraint.

By 2000, universal suffrage across the League's founding and early member states is old enough to have stopped being a live question in any of them. The debates each state had — over land, over birth, over trade, over registration, over competence — are constitutional history now, taught rather than argued. What is taught consistently, across every League member's own account of its own suffrage history, is that the fight was never really about who was born a citizen. It was about what a state was willing to accept as proof that a person had earned a voice in its own governance — and on that question, four different republics arrived at four different answers, and then spent three centuries watching each other's answers and revising their own.
