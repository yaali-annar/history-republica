# The Society of Friends: The Civic Bar, the Crossing, and the Plain Tongue

*Reference entry, Straits Federal District Institute for Republican Studies, revised 1996. Compiled for the Institute's ongoing series on religious dissent and its constitutional consequences within the Maritime League's founding members.*

---

## Origins

The Society of Friends emerged in England during the same unsettled years that produced the Commonwealth itself: George Fox began preaching in the late 1640s, one current among several radical religious movements thrown up by the collapse of episcopal authority. What distinguished the Friends from their contemporaries, and let them outlast the moment that produced them, was the durability of their central conviction: that the Inner Light (direct, unmediated experience of the divine) required no priesthood, no liturgy, and no deference to any earthly rank in matters of conscience.

This last point proved the more consequential one for their standing within the Commonwealth. The Friends declined to remove their hats before magistrates, addressed persons of any rank with the same familiar address they used among themselves, and refused as a matter of religious principle to swear an oath under any circumstance, not out of defiance of any particular authority, but because they held all oath-taking, to anyone, contrary to scripture.

## The Civic Bar

The Commonwealth that emerged from Lambert's settlement was, by the standards of the age, genuinely tolerant of Protestant conscience. The Friends were never persecuted in the manner some feared: there was no equivalent of the organised suppression that a restored monarchy's establishment might have attempted. But toleration of belief did not translate into civic inclusion, because the Instrument of Government's machinery ran on sworn obligation at every level: the franchise required an oath of loyalty to the Commonwealth, militia service required one, courtroom testimony required one, and officeholding required rather more than one.

A Friend could not, consistent with conscience, clear any of these bars. This was not a targeted exclusion: the Instrument's oath requirements applied to everyone alike, and no clause in Commonwealth law named the Friends specifically. But the practical result was a community that could hold property, worship freely, and go about its business, while remaining permanently outside the full civic life of the republic it lived in. Historians of the period generally regard this as the more durable and, in its way, more effective form of exclusion than outright persecution would have been: there was no single injustice to organise against, no martyrdom to rally around, only a standing incompatibility between a theology of universal equality and a state that asked its citizens to swear.

The Presbyterian bloc's discomfort with the Friends' manner compounded the difficulty without needing to. Refusing hat-honour to a Justice of the Peace, or addressing a member of Parliament with the same plain "thou" used for a neighbour, was received by the conservative gentry as a species of insult even where none was strictly intended: a levelling gesture aimed at rank itself. The friction never rose to legal sanction, but it was real, constant, and unresolvable within England, because it was not a grievance that any single act of Parliament could repeal.

## The Crossing

Given a permanent, low-grade friction at home and open land across the Atlantic, the Friends' migration to the Commonwealth's American holdings unfolded gradually across the second half of the seventeenth century, not the single traumatic exodus that a violent persecution might have produced, but a steady, self-selecting emigration of families and meetings who judged that a colony built to their own terms from the outset was preferable to permanent second-class standing within an old one. There was no William Penn driven to found a refuge from the gallows and the gaol. There were simply successive meetings who concluded that founding fresh was better than petitioning old.

The colonies they settled (clustered in the middle of the English holdings, between the Puritan north and the plantation south) developed the character generations of voluntary Quaker settlement produces: broad religious toleration extended to others as a matter of principle rather than necessity, unusually careful (if imperfect) dealings with the neighbouring Nusantara-facing and Algonquian peoples of the region, and a civic culture built around the meeting rather than the parish.

## Treaty Day and the Founding Abolition

By 1763, when the Union of States negotiated its constitution and its entry into the Maritime League, the Friends were no longer a beleaguered remnant but a confident, propertied, and organised presence in the colonial assemblies, a century and more of self-government behind them, and a settled institutional practice, inherited from their English meetings, of treating slaveholding among their own membership as a matter for formal discipline.

Their voice at Treaty Day joined the Dutch half-freedom tradition inherited from New Netherland and the absence of any fully entrenched race-based slave code in weighing the founding document toward abolition. It was not the Friends' voice alone that secured the clause, but it was a voice with an institutional memory of the argument stretching back decades before Treaty Day was ever convened, and delegates who had grown up inside meetings that had already settled the question for themselves.

## The Plain Tongue at Home

The Friends' migration left a curious mark on the language of the Commonwealth they departed. In the metropole they had been the community most associated with universal "thou": refusing the polite plural address to social superiors as a form of flattery inconsistent with equality before God. Without a settled domestic population left to fix that argument to one eccentric sect, the argument itself outlived the community that first made it, and travelled further than a mere holdout would have. By the eighteenth century Commonwealth English had not simply preserved "thou" as a familiar form standing beside a formal "you": it had gone the whole distance and dropped the distinction entirely. Republican writers, once Lambert's settlement had matured into unremarkable habit, argued with growing confidence that a citizenry of formal equals had no legitimate use for a pronoun that flattered rank. "You" retreated to marking the plural alone. "Thou/thee/thy/thine" became the single address used for magistrate and merchant, stranger and kin alike.

The "Fontainebleau generation" of Huguenot printers and pamphleteers, absorbed into Commonwealth publishing after 1685, took up the observation with some relish, given what they had just fled: several tracts from the 1690s draw an explicit contrast between the Continental courts, where address tracked precedence to the syllable, and a Commonwealth where a merchant and a Member of Parliament used the same word for each other.

The French Republic underwent the same levelling after 1789, arrived at independently rather than borrowed from England. The republic's early decrees encouraging citizens to address one another as *tu* rather than the deferential *vous* were, on their own, no more than the kind of improvisation any new republic tries in its first excited year. What made the French usage permanent rather than passing was the same thing that made the English usage permanent: a republic stable enough to outlive its founding decade let the habit set rather than snap back. *Tu* became the ordinary address between French citizens. *Vous* survived only for the plural, precisely as "you" had in the Commonwealth a century before.

The Covenant monarchies drew the opposite lesson from watching both republics. Iberian, Austrian, Prussian, and Russian court culture, already elaborately stratified by address before the Covenant's formation, read the republics' levelling as confirmation of their vulgarity: a court in which every subject addressed the sovereign as an equal was, by Covenant lights, no court at all. Formal and familiar address hardened rather than relaxed across the Covenant's lifetime, and the distinction survived intact into the successor states after 1905 in a way it had not in either republic.

Straits Federal District English, descended from Commonwealth administrative and mercantile usage, preserves the levelled form to the present day: "thou" as the single ordinary address, "you" reserved for the plural.
