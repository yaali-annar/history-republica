# Java, Sumatra, and the Shape of Nusantara

This piece traces how the Dutch Republic's 1641 withdrawal from land ambitions in Java (see `world/17th-century.md`) shaped the political geography of the archipelago — why the Nusantara Federation formed where it did, why two of the region's most powerful states sit outside it, and how that split set the stage for a maritime alliance that reached as far as the southern continent.

---

## The Absence of a Land Empire

The Dutch Republic's decision in the 1640s to abandon territorial conquest in Java — trading a land empire it could not afford for a secured commercial position at Batam — left the island's politics to run on their own logic for the next two and a half centuries. There was no VOC to exploit succession disputes, no forced-cultivation system to reorganise Javanese agriculture around export crops, no colonial administration to eventually inherit. **Mataram**, the dominant power in Java since the early seventeenth century, was never subjected to the kind of external intervention that split it into rival courts elsewhere. It entered the modern era as a single, unified, formidable state — the largest independent civilisation in the Nusantara region and, by the nineteenth century, one of the few Asian polities of its size never to fall under Western administration in any form.

Mataram's independence was not incidental to the Federation's later shape. It was the fact the Federation formed around.

The absence of a colonial arbiter should not be read as an absence of conflict. Elsewhere, a colonial power positioned to exploit a court's succession disputes typically ends them as much as provokes them, imposing partition on its own terms once a quarrel serves its interests. Mataram had no such outside power holding that kind of leverage over it, and its succession politics ran without anyone capable of forcing a settlement. The unified Mataram that survives to the modern era is not a state that avoided civil war; it is the outcome of one court eventually winning a long and genuinely internal struggle, fought to exhaustion rather than frozen by treaty. The archipelago outside the Federation is not a peaceful alternative history — it is a harder and more openly contested one, self-determined precisely because nothing stopped it from being violent.

## Banten, Sunda, and the Older Rivalry

To Mataram's west, the Sultanate of **Banten** anchored the Sunda-speaking lands of western Java — a distinct people from the Javanese of the central and eastern courts, with a rivalry that long predates any European presence in the archipelago. Banten's alignment, when the Nusantara Federation began to take shape in the nineteenth century, followed that older fault line rather than any calculation about the Maritime League. A federation that gave Sunda an institutional counterweight to Javanese power was worth joining on its own terms; the League's commercial nudging (see below) gave the impulse a channel, not its cause.

## The League's Nudge

The Maritime League's clearing houses at Batam and Tumasik had spent a century and a half negotiating separately with a field of sultanates across the archipelago — the same friction, at larger scale, that had once made a fragmented Borneo interior an unattractive trading partner before the Lanfang-Fosjoen merger. A single federated signature was worth more to the League than dozens of individual courts, each with its own succession politics and none bound by the others' commitments. The League did not compel federation. It priced coordination favourably — better credit, better shipping terms, preferential treatment for sultanates willing to negotiate jointly — and let the arithmetic do the work over decades, the same mechanism that had consolidated Lanfang.

The sultanates that had the most to gain from this arrangement were the ones exposed to Covenant pressure: the Malay sultanates of the peninsula and Sumatra facing the Sulu raiding networks and Iberian patronage out of the Philippines, and — critically — the northern reach of Sulawesi, hard against the Iberian colonial border. The result, by the time the Federation was constituted around 1850, was a body encompassing Sumatra, Borneo, the Malay Peninsula, Banten's Sunda-speaking west Java, and northern Sulawesi: a Federation with no ethnic-Javanese constituent state and no dominant single population group, making Malay — already the archipelago's trade lingua franca — its uncontested political and administrative language.

## Aceh, Mataram, and the Bloc Outside

Two powers saw no reason to join.

**Aceh**, dominant on the northern approach to the Malacca Straits, had never been conquered, never needed League protection from a Covenant threat it was fully capable of meeting itself, and had nothing to gain from folding its sovereignty into a federation of lesser courts. It remained fully independent, a third pole in the Straits alongside the Federation and the Federal District.

**Mataram**, similarly, had no Covenant exposure to manage on its western and southern flanks — its problem lay to the east, where Iberian **Timor** sat astride the Lesser Sunda chain as the Covenant's forward position in the archipelago, backed by the same patronage network that armed Sulu raiding fleets against Lanfang. Mataram's naval and diplomatic energy through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries went into containing and contesting Timor, not into any federal project to its west.

What gave Mataram's ambitions their reach was an alignment with the **Bugis and Makassar sultanates** of southern Sulawesi — the archipelago's premier seafarers, whose ships and captains had carried Bugis communities and dynasties across the Malay world for generations. Where northern Sulawesi's exposure to the Philippines pulled it toward League association, southern Sulawesi's free-port trading tradition and lack of any comparable exposure kept it outside the Federation and gave it every reason to partner with a land power that could offer manpower and agrarian wealth in exchange for ships. The Mataram-Bugis-Makassar axis became, in effect, the Federation's southern counterpart: a second bloc, non-aligned with the League, commanding the eastern archipelago's contested waters and — eventually — the trade and settlement networks reaching further south still.

## Toward the Southern Continent

The Bugis and Makassar mariners of this alliance were not new to the waters south of Timor and the Arafura Sea; their trepang fishing fleets had worked the northern coast of the southern continent and traded with its peoples for generations before the alliance with Mataram existed in any formal sense. What the Mataram partnership added was the reach to turn a seasonal fishing and trading contact into permanent settlement — a project that gathered force, in part, as compensation for the stalemate at Timor: blocked from securing the easier eastward path through the Lesser Sundas, the Mataram court's ambitions bent southward instead, along a route that ran wide of Covenant waters entirely.

The fuller history of that settlement — its founding, its relationship with the southern continent's indigenous peoples, and its consequences for the Mataram-Bugis-Makassar bloc's internal balance — is not yet written.
